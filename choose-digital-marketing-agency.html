How to choose a digital marketing agency

Choosing a marketing partner is a business decision, not simply a design contest or a comparison of monthly fees. The right provider should understand the organisation's goals, explain the work clearly, report honestly and agree on responsibilities before a campaign begins.

Published 20 November 2026 · Editorial team: Vijes Digital Links

Digital marketing agencies can offer a wide range of services, including strategy, search marketing, social media, content, email campaigns, paid advertising, website development and analytics. Some specialise in one channel; others coordinate several disciplines. The best fit depends on the problem a business needs to solve and the capabilities it already has internally.

The buyer lesson is to look beyond a polished presentation. Ask a prospective agency to explain the client's starting problem, its exact responsibilities, the test plan, reporting cadence, how results were measured and which factors were outside its control. Ask for access to the underlying accounts and data needed to verify work.

What to apply: A defined pilot, agreed KPIs, regular reporting, clear ownership of accounts and honest discussion of limitations are stronger signs of a sound partnership than a promise of guaranteed rankings or sales.

The Baringa example is an anonymised, consultant-published case study. Use it as a model for questions to ask, not as proof that every agency engagement will deliver similar percentage increases.

This guide provides a structured way to evaluate providers. It is not an endorsement of any particular agency, and a polished website or confident sales presentation should not replace due diligence.

1. Define the business problem first

Before contacting agencies, write down what needs to change. Examples include improving the quality of sales enquiries, launching a new service, making a website easier to use, communicating with existing customers or measuring campaign performance more reliably.

Identify the audience, geography, timing, available budget and internal resources. Decide what the provider will be expected to do and what your own team will handle. A clear brief helps agencies propose comparable work and reduces the risk of paying for services that do not address the actual need.

2. Match the provider's capabilities to the work

Do not assume that every agency is equally strong in every discipline. Ask who will perform the work and what experience or process they bring to the specific assignment.

  • Strategy: Can the provider connect activity to a business objective and explain the reasoning behind the proposed approach?
  • Content: Can the team research, write, edit and maintain material that is accurate and appropriate for the intended audience?
  • Social media: Can it plan content, manage publishing, respond within agreed boundaries and report on relevant outcomes?
  • Email marketing: Does it understand permission, list hygiene, sender authentication, deliverability and unsubscribe handling?
  • Paid advertising: Can it explain campaign structure, budget controls, targeting, conversion measurement and account access?
  • Website work: Does it address mobile usability, accessibility, security, performance, content management and ongoing maintenance?
  • Research and analytics: Can it explain sources, methods, assumptions and limitations rather than presenting unsupported conclusions?

Ask whether the work is performed by the agency's own team, approved contractors or other partners. The arrangement is not automatically good or bad, but the provider should explain who is responsible and how quality is checked.

3. Ask for relevant evidence, not just impressive claims

Request examples that are relevant to your organisation's challenge. A useful example explains the starting situation, the work completed, the time period, the measurement method and the result. Ask what factors may have influenced the outcome and whether the provider can substantiate the figures.

Some clients cannot be named because of confidentiality. In that case, a provider may be able to share an anonymised example or demonstrate its process. Treat claims such as “guaranteed first place,” “instant viral growth” or “guaranteed sales” with caution. Marketing outcomes depend on factors beyond an agency's control, including the offer, market conditions, budget, competition and the client's own operations.

Check references where possible and with the relevant person's permission. A testimonial is useful context, but it should not be treated as proof that the same outcome will occur for every client.

4. Compare proposals on the same basis

Ask each shortlisted provider to respond to the same brief. A proposal should explain the problem understood, recommended scope, deliverables, schedule, assumptions, client responsibilities, fees and exclusions.

Compare the substance of the offer rather than only the total price. One proposal may include research, content production, revisions and reporting, while another may cover only account management or publishing. Ask for clarification when an item is vague.

For a retainer, establish what work is included each month, how priorities are agreed, what happens to unused hours or deliverables and how additional work is priced. For a project fee, identify milestones, acceptance criteria and the process for approving changes.

5. Understand measurement and reporting

Agree on what the provider will measure before work begins. Metrics should connect to the objective and be defined clearly. If the goal is qualified enquiries, a report focused only on followers and impressions will not answer the main business question.

Ask how often reports are provided, which tools supply the data, who can access the accounts and how the team will explain weak or unexpected results. A responsible provider should be able to discuss limitations, not only positive outcomes.

Clarify how leads are defined and whether the agency is responsible for campaign activity only or also for sales follow-up. The agency may generate enquiries, but the client's response time, pricing, service quality and sales process can influence whether those enquiries become customers.

6. Keep ownership and access clear

Before granting access, agree who owns the website, domain, advertising accounts, social pages, mailing lists, analytics properties, creative files and campaign data. Where possible, accounts should be created in the client's organisation and the provider should receive role-based access needed for the work.

Do not rely on an agency's personal email address as the only administrator of a critical business asset. Use secure access controls, multi-factor authentication where available and a documented process for removing access when a contract ends.

Clarify whether the client receives editable source files, design assets, copy, campaign structures, reports and configuration details at the end of the engagement. Check whether any third-party licences or subscriptions must continue to use the work.

7. Review the contract and data-handling arrangements

Read the agreement before paying or sharing customer information. It should identify the parties, scope, fees, payment schedule, timeline, revision limits, approval process, confidentiality, intellectual property, data handling, termination and dispute process.

Ask how the provider will access, store, transfer and delete customer or employee information. Confirm whether subcontractors are involved and what safeguards apply. Share only the minimum data necessary for the agreed task, and use appropriate agreements and professional advice where personal data or regulated information is involved.

For international projects, clarify the contracting entity, currency, applicable taxes, payment charges, governing law and any cross-border data-transfer requirements. Seek qualified legal or tax advice when the implications are material.

8. Start with a defined pilot when uncertainty is high

If you are unsure about the working relationship, consider a small paid pilot with a clear output and review point. It might involve an audit, a short research assignment, a content package or a limited campaign test.

A pilot should have a defined scope, price, timeline, acceptance criteria and access requirements. It should test the provider's communication and delivery process as well as the quality of the work. Do not treat a short pilot as proof that long-term commercial results are guaranteed.

9. Warning signs to investigate

  • Guaranteed rankings, sales or viral results without explaining assumptions or risks.
  • Pressure to pay immediately or refusal to provide a written scope.
  • Vague deliverables, hidden fees or unclear cancellation terms.
  • Requests for passwords instead of secure, limited account access.
  • Refusal to explain who will perform the work or how results are measured.
  • Unverifiable testimonials, client logos, awards or performance statistics.
  • Promises to use purchased email lists, fake engagement or misleading advertising.
  • Reluctance to explain ownership of accounts, content or data when the contract ends.

One warning sign does not automatically prove misconduct, but it is a reason to pause, ask questions and obtain independent advice before proceeding.

10. A practical agency evaluation scorecard

Score each shortlisted provider against the same criteria. Use a simple scale such as 1 (weak evidence) to 5 (strong evidence), and write a note explaining each score. Do not allow a high presentation score to compensate for missing contractual or security safeguards.

  • Understanding of the business problem and target audience.
  • Relevant skills and a credible delivery process.
  • Evidence quality and transparency about limitations.
  • Clarity of deliverables, timeline and responsibilities.
  • Measurement plan and reporting approach.
  • Pricing transparency and overall value for the defined scope.
  • Ownership, security and data-handling arrangements.
  • Contract terms, communication and exit arrangements.
Questions to ask before signing: What exactly will you deliver? Who will do the work? What will we need to provide? How will success be measured? Which accounts and files will we own? What is excluded from the fee? How can either party end the agreement?
