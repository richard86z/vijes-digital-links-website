INTERNATIONAL BUSINESS · PRACTICAL GUIDE

How to build an international digital presence

Reaching customers in other countries takes more than adding a “global” message to a website. Businesses need to choose the right markets, explain their value clearly, build trust across borders and make it easy for interested people to take the next step.

Published 20 November 2026 · Editorial team: Vijes Digital Links

An international digital presence is the combination of information, channels and customer experiences that allow people in different markets to discover, understand and contact a business. It may include a website, search listings, social channels, email communication, online marketplaces and partner networks.

Evidence from real international expansion cases

Amara: localising the customer experience for France

Source date: 21 July 2021 (Lionbridge case study update). Amara, a UK homeware retailer, launched a French-language website after a year of planning. Lionbridge reported that, within 45 days of launch, traffic from France was up 700%, SEO traffic from France was up 500%, conversion rate had increased 20%, and revenue from France was up 650% year on year. This is a provider-published case study; the figures are reported by Lionbridge and should not be treated as independently audited or as proof that translation alone caused every change. The useful lesson is that language, search visibility and a purchase journey customers can understand need to work together. Read the Amara case study (updated 21 July 2021).

The Shirt Company: language, currency and landed cost

Source date: 25 November 2021 (Glopal case study). The Shirt Company added language localisation, currency conversion and checkout calculations for duties and taxes. Glopal reported a 68% increase in international sales in the first 30 days and a 3.5-times increase after 90 days. These are Glopal-published client results, not an independent controlled study. The practical point is that international buyers need to understand not only the product description but also the amount they will pay and the conditions of delivery. Read The Shirt Company case study (25 November 2021).

Research takeaway: Test a complete local buyer journey—language, currency display, shipping, taxes, payment and support—rather than assuming a translated homepage is enough.

The examples above are historical case studies. Results vary by market, product, pricing, fulfilment, competition and execution; they are not forecasts for Vijes Digital Links or any other business.

A business does not need to enter every country at once. A focused approach is usually easier to manage: identify a market where there is a credible reason to compete, learn what buyers need, make the offer understandable and test whether the business can serve enquiries reliably.

1. Choose a market for a reason

Start with a specific customer group or business problem rather than a long list of countries. A market may be worth researching because existing customers operate there, buyers are actively searching for the service, a partner has relevant reach, or the business has a capability that is difficult to find locally.

Use a simple market-screening table. Record the evidence behind each rating instead of relying on assumptions.

  • Demand: What evidence suggests that people or organisations in this market need the offer?
  • Customer fit: Does the business understand the likely buyer, decision process and expectations?
  • Competition: Which alternatives do buyers already use, and how will the offer be meaningfully different?
  • Ability to deliver: Can the business meet expected service levels, time zones, language needs and support requirements?
  • Commercial practicality: Can the business price, invoice, receive payment and manage tax or regulatory obligations appropriately?

Public search results, industry directories, trade publications, competitor websites, customer interviews and small surveys can help build an initial picture. Treat online search interest as a clue, not proof that a market will produce paying customers. Verify important assumptions with prospective buyers or qualified local advisers.

2. Define the buyer before choosing the channel

Different buyers look for different information. A procurement manager may need supplier credentials, service scope, delivery timelines and a formal proposal. A small business owner may want clear packages, examples of deliverables and a simple way to request a consultation. A consumer may prioritise price, reviews, shipping and returns.

Write a short buyer profile that answers these questions:

  1. Who is the buyer, and what role do they play in the decision?
  2. What problem are they trying to solve?
  3. What would make them trust a new provider?
  4. What information do they need before making contact?
  5. What could prevent them from proceeding?

Use real conversations, enquiry records and feedback to refine the profile. Do not present guesses about a country's culture or buying habits as facts about everyone in that market.

3. Make the website understandable to an international visitor

Your website is often the place where a potential customer checks whether your organisation is credible. A visitor should be able to understand what you offer, who you serve, where you can operate and how to contact you without searching through several pages.

Review the following areas:

  • Clear positioning: State the service, intended customer and value in plain language.
  • Specific service pages: Explain what is included, the typical process, what the client must provide and what the service does not promise.
  • Contact details: Provide a working contact route and explain when visitors can expect a response.
  • Trust information: Publish accurate business information, privacy terms, service terms and any relevant qualifications or registrations.
  • Mobile usability: Check that pages, forms, menus and contact links work on a phone as well as a desktop.
  • Accessible presentation: Use readable text, meaningful headings, descriptive links and alternative text for informative images.

Do not imply that an office, local team, certification, client relationship or country-level capability exists unless it can be substantiated. It is better to state the actual service arrangement clearly than to create an impression that may disappoint a customer later.

4. Adapt the message without changing the truth

International communication should be relevant to the audience while remaining accurate. Adapt examples, spelling conventions, date formats, currency references and terminology where they genuinely help the reader. Explain unfamiliar abbreviations and avoid slang that may not translate well.

Localisation is more than translating words. A translated page should preserve the intended meaning, calls to action, service conditions and legal notices. For important contracts, regulated information or sensitive communications, use a qualified language professional and appropriate legal review rather than relying only on automated translation.

Be transparent about pricing. If a price depends on scope, location, taxes, delivery or currency conversion, explain how a quote is prepared. Do not show a currency symbol that could be interpreted differently in different countries without naming the currency code.

5. Select a manageable set of digital channels

Choose channels according to how the target audience discovers and evaluates providers. A business-to-business service company might use a clear website, useful search-focused articles, professional networking and carefully researched direct outreach. A consumer brand may need a different mix, such as social content, marketplace listings, customer reviews and email updates.

Start with one main destination—usually the website—and a small number of channels that can be maintained consistently. Each channel should have a purpose:

  • Website: Explain the offer, answer common questions and convert relevant interest into enquiries.
  • Search: Help people find specific service or educational pages when they are actively researching a need.
  • Social media: Demonstrate expertise, share updates and take part in relevant professional conversations.
  • Email: Communicate with people who have given permission to receive updates or marketing messages.
  • Partnerships: Build relationships with organisations that serve a compatible audience and have a clear reason to collaborate.

A channel that produces many views but no relevant conversations may not be the best use of limited time. Review the quality of attention, not just the volume.

6. Build trust before asking for a sale

People considering an unfamiliar provider need enough information to assess risk. Explain how enquiries are handled, how work is scoped, who is responsible for communication, how changes are approved and what happens if a project needs to be paused or revised.

Use genuine proof only. This can include work samples you have permission to show, clearly labelled demonstrations, accurate credentials, published policies and testimonials that reflect real customer experiences and have been authorised for use. If you are new to a market and do not yet have local case studies, say so. Do not invent client logos, reviews, results or partnerships.

Make the next step low-friction. A contact form should request only the information needed to respond. Tell visitors what happens after they submit it. Keep privacy notices accessible and handle personal information in line with applicable data-protection requirements.

7. Prepare the operation behind the marketing

Promotion can create demand that the business is not ready to serve. Before expanding outreach, check the operational details that affect the customer experience:

  • Who monitors enquiries, and how quickly can the team respond?
  • How will the business confirm the customer's requirements and eligibility?
  • Which time zones and languages can the team support?
  • How are proposals, contracts, invoices and payment confirmations handled?
  • How will delivery progress, revisions and complaints be managed?
  • Which local rules, tax obligations, consumer protections or licensing requirements need professional advice?

Document the process so that the customer receives consistent information. If a service cannot be delivered in a particular location, state that limitation early instead of accepting an enquiry that cannot be fulfilled.

8. Measure whether the market is responding

Set a baseline before launching a campaign. Then review a small group of measures that connect activity to business outcomes:

  • Relevant visits: Are people from the intended market reaching the right pages?
  • Enquiry rate: What proportion of relevant visitors submit a genuine enquiry?
  • Lead quality: How many enquiries match the service, budget, location and delivery criteria?
  • Response and conversion: How quickly does the business respond, and how many qualified enquiries progress to a proposal or sale?
  • Cost and capacity: What does it cost to generate and serve a customer, and can the team deliver profitably?

Use analytics and customer records responsibly. Explain relevant tracking in your privacy notice, obtain consent where required and avoid collecting information that is not needed. Review trends over a meaningful period; a small number of visits or enquiries can fluctuate significantly from week to week.

9. A practical 30-day starting plan

This sample schedule is a planning aid, not a guarantee of international sales. Adjust it to the team's capacity and the complexity of the service.

  1. Week 1 — Research: Select one target market and one buyer group. Gather evidence about demand, competitors, buyer questions and delivery requirements.
  2. Week 2 — Prepare: Improve the most relevant website page, clarify the offer and contact process, and prepare accurate trust and policy information.
  3. Week 3 — Test: Publish one useful piece of market-relevant content and begin a small, carefully targeted outreach or partnership test.
  4. Week 4 — Review: Assess relevant visits, responses, lead quality, questions and operational effort. Decide whether to refine the message, change the channel or continue testing.
Market-entry worksheet: Before spending on promotion, write down (1) the market and buyer you are targeting, (2) the evidence of demand, (3) the problem your offer solves, (4) the proof you can honestly provide, (5) the channel you will test, (6) the delivery requirements and (7) the measure that will determine whether the test was worthwhile.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Trying to target every country and audience at the same time.
  • Assuming website visits automatically mean commercial demand.
  • Using generic claims such as “number one” or “world-class” without evidence.
  • Copying competitors' wording instead of explaining the organisation's own offer.
  • Launching campaigns before the team can respond to and fulfil enquiries.
  • Using purchased or scraped contact lists, or sending unsolicited bulk email.
  • Publishing fake testimonials, client logos, case studies or local addresses.
  • Ignoring payment, privacy, tax, consumer-protection or licensing requirements in target markets.

Practical worksheet: compare two target markets

Use this lightweight scorecard before committing budget. Score each market from 1 (weak or unknown) to 5 (strong evidence). These are planning scores, not market facts.

  1. Customer demand: What evidence shows that the problem exists? Record search patterns, enquiries, interviews or category activity.
  2. Ability to serve: Can you deliver reliably across time zones, language, payment, support and fulfilment?
  3. Competition: Identify three alternatives and compare their offer, pricing, proof and customer experience.
  4. Economics: Estimate acquisition cost, delivery cost, taxes/fees and realistic contribution margin.
  5. Trust and compliance: List required disclosures, privacy obligations, consumer rules and proof a buyer expects.

Decision record: Market ___; strongest evidence ___; biggest unknown ___; cheapest test ___; owner ___; review date ___. Do not enter a market solely because its population is large. Validate a narrow customer segment and a specific offer first.
