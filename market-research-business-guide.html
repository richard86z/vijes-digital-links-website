BUSINESS RESEARCH · PRACTICAL GUIDE

How to conduct market research for a business

Good market research helps a business make a better decision before committing time and money. It does not remove uncertainty, but it can reveal what customers need, how alternatives are positioned and which assumptions deserve a closer test.

Published 20 November 2026 · Editorial team: Vijes Digital Links

Market research is a structured way to collect and interpret information about customers, competitors, market conditions and potential demand. It can support decisions such as which service to launch, which customer group to prioritise, how to position an offer, what price to test or whether a new location is worth exploring.

The most useful research begins with a decision. Collecting information without knowing what it will help you decide can create a large folder of facts without a clear business outcome.

1. Start with the decision, not the survey

Write down the business decision you need to make in one sentence. Then identify what you do not yet know. For example, “Should we introduce a monthly social media support package for independent professional firms?” leads to more useful research questions than “What do people think about social media?”

Turn the decision into a small set of answerable questions:

  • Which type of organisation experiences the problem most often?
  • How do potential customers currently solve it?
  • What is frustrating, expensive or missing about existing options?
  • Who approves the purchase and what criteria do they use?
  • What budget range or commercial model is realistic?
  • What evidence would make us proceed, revise the offer or stop?

Separate facts you already know from assumptions you need to test. This prevents an early opinion from quietly becoming the conclusion.

2. Define the market and customer group

A market can be segmented by industry, organisation size, geography, customer type, use case or purchasing behaviour. Choose characteristics that affect the need or the way a buyer makes a decision. “All businesses” is usually too broad to guide a useful study.

Write a short research profile covering the target group, the problem being investigated, the relevant geography and the period of interest. If the study concerns international demand, do not assume that results from one country automatically apply to another. Language, regulation, payment practices, competition and customer expectations may differ.

3. Combine secondary and primary research

Secondary research uses information that already exists. Examples include official statistics, regulator publications, trade association reports, company websites, public procurement notices, industry publications and published research. It can help estimate market context and identify questions to investigate further.

Primary research collects information directly for the decision at hand. Methods include customer interviews, surveys, observation, usability tests, pilot offers and structured conversations with industry participants. Primary research can provide specific insight, but its quality depends on who participates, how questions are asked and how responses are interpreted.

Use both where practical. Secondary sources can show the broader picture; conversations with prospective buyers can explain how a problem is experienced in practice. Record the source, date, geography and limitations of important information so that another person can understand where a conclusion came from.

4. Design interviews that uncover behaviour

Interviews are useful when you need to understand the reasons behind a decision. Speak with people who genuinely fit the target group, and explain the purpose of the conversation. Avoid leading questions that suggest the answer you want to hear.

Questions about recent behaviour are often more revealing than asking someone to predict what they might buy. Consider asking:

  • Tell me about the last time you tried to solve this problem.
  • What options did you consider?
  • What made you choose the option you used?
  • What was difficult, slow or disappointing?
  • Who else was involved in the decision?
  • What would need to change before you considered another solution?

Ask follow-up questions for examples. With permission, take notes or record the discussion securely. Do not publish a participant's name, quotation or organisation without appropriate permission.

5. Build a survey that can produce usable answers

Use a survey when you need comparable answers from a larger group or want to measure how common a finding may be within a defined audience. A survey is not automatically representative just because many people respond. Who received it, who chose to answer and how questions were worded all affect the result.

Keep the questionnaire focused. Use one idea per question, define time periods clearly and provide response options that do not force people into an inaccurate answer. Avoid double-barrelled questions such as “Is the service affordable and easy to use?” because a respondent may feel differently about affordability and usability.

Test the survey with a few people before wider distribution. Check whether they understand the wording, whether the options cover their likely answers and how long completion takes. Explain who is collecting the information, why it is needed and how responses will be used. Collect only information necessary for the study and handle personal data responsibly.

6. Analyse competitors by comparing the same things

Competitor research should go beyond copying visible prices or listing company names. Identify direct alternatives, indirect alternatives and the possibility that a customer may decide to do nothing or handle the task internally.

Create a comparison using consistent criteria:

  • Target customer and problem addressed.
  • Services, features or deliverables included.
  • Published price, pricing model or quote process.
  • Evidence of quality, expertise or customer support.
  • Delivery area, availability and limitations.
  • Customer feedback themes and recurring complaints.
  • What appears genuinely different about the offer.

Use publicly available information accurately and respect website terms and privacy requirements. A competitor's marketing claim is evidence of how it positions itself, not independent proof that the claim is true. Record the date you reviewed the information because offers and prices change.

7. Check the quality of your evidence

Before drawing conclusions, ask whether the evidence is current, relevant and sufficiently reliable for the decision. Look for the original source rather than repeating an unsourced statistic from another website. Check the geography, sample, methodology and date of any report or survey.

Be alert to common research problems:

  • Selection bias: Participants differ from the wider group you want to understand.
  • Leading questions: Wording nudges respondents towards a preferred answer.
  • Small or narrow samples: Findings are treated as universal despite limited coverage.
  • Outdated information: Old pricing, policy or market figures are presented as current.
  • Confirmation bias: Evidence supporting the original idea receives more attention than contradictory evidence.
  • Correlation mistaken for cause: Two things occur together, but the research has not shown that one caused the other.

State uncertainty openly. If a sample is small or drawn from one network, describe it as exploratory feedback rather than a representative picture of the whole market.

8. Turn findings into a decision

A research report should help someone act. Start with the original decision, summarise the strongest findings, show the evidence behind them and explain what remains unknown. Separate observed facts, participant opinions and your interpretation.

A concise findings table can make the logic clear:

  • Finding: What did the research indicate?
  • Evidence: Which source, observation or response supports it?
  • Confidence: How strong and consistent is the evidence?
  • Business implication: Why does it matter?
  • Next action: What should be tested, changed or investigated?

Recommendations should match the strength of the evidence. A small exploratory study may justify a limited pilot, not a large investment or a claim that demand has been proven.

9. A practical two-week research sprint

  1. Days 1–2 — Frame the question: Agree on the decision, target group, geography, assumptions and research questions.
  2. Days 3–5 — Review existing evidence: Gather relevant official, industry and competitor information. Record links, dates and limitations.
  3. Days 6–9 — Hear from the audience: Conduct a small set of relevant interviews or test a short survey. Keep the method appropriate to the question.
  4. Days 10–11 — Compare and analyse: Group responses into themes, compare alternatives and note contradictions or gaps.
  5. Days 12–14 — Report and decide: Present findings, confidence levels, risks and recommended next steps. Identify what should be validated in a pilot.

The schedule is an example for a focused project. Research involving multiple countries, regulated sectors, large samples or complex procurement decisions may require more time and specialist support.

Research brief checklist: Before starting, document the decision to be made, target audience, geography, research questions, sources and methods, participant approach, privacy arrangements, timeline, budget and the evidence that would change the decision.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting with a questionnaire before defining the business decision.
  • Asking friends or existing contacts and treating their views as representative of an entire market.
  • Using old or unverified statistics without checking their original source.
  • Confusing interest, likes or stated intentions with actual purchasing behaviour.
  • Ignoring feedback that challenges the proposed idea.
  • Collecting personal information without a clear purpose or appropriate safeguards.
  • Producing a long report that does not state what the business should do next.
