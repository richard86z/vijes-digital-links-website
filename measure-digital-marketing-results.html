How to measure digital marketing results

Marketing reports are useful when they explain what happened, why it may have happened and what the organisation should do next. A dashboard full of numbers is not enough: measurement needs to connect activity to a clear business objective.

Published 20 November 2026 · Editorial team: Vijes Digital Links

Digital marketing measurement is the process of assessing whether online activity is helping an organisation reach its goals. Depending on the objective, that may mean improving awareness among a defined audience, attracting qualified website visits, generating enquiries, increasing online sales, retaining customers or encouraging event registrations.

Evidence that better measurement can change campaign decisions

Publicis, Spark Foundry and OptiMine: measure across the funnel

Source date: 27 January 2022 (OptiMine case study). Spark Foundry worked with measurement partner OptiMine on campaigns for a large U.S. supplemental health insurer. The case study describes using cross-channel measurement to understand how upper-funnel advertising influenced lower-funnel conversions, including activity across digital, mobile, call centres and agents. It reports a 14.05% increase in conversions with 4.91% additional investment and an 8.01% decrease in cost per lead. These are vendor-published case-study figures; the client and analytics provider reported the results, so they should not be presented as an independently audited causal estimate. Read the OptiMine case study (27 January 2022).

Netflix: use controlled tests as a release safeguard

Source date: 12 February 2024 (Netflix Technology Blog). Netflix engineers described software “canary” A/B tests that compare a current version with a proposed version using a limited audience and a key experience measure—time from pressing play to playback beginning. The purpose is to detect regressions early and prevent a release from reaching everyone when performance worsens. This is a technical experimentation example rather than an advertising-sales case, but it demonstrates the value of defining a measurable outcome and testing before broad rollout. Read Netflix's A/B testing article (12 February 2024).

What to apply: Define the business outcome and its metric before a campaign starts. Use supporting indicators to explain the result, check attribution limits and record what decision the data changed.

No single metric explains every campaign. A high number of views may indicate reach, but it does not prove that the right people saw the message or took a valuable action. The first step is to decide what success means for the specific activity.

1. Connect each campaign to a business objective

Begin with the business outcome, then identify the marketing action that could contribute to it. For example, if the objective is to generate enquiries for a professional service, the campaign might direct a defined audience to a service page and invite them to submit a project brief.

Write the objective in a way that can be evaluated. “Improve social media” is too vague. “Increase relevant visits to the consulting service page and generate qualified consultation requests during the campaign period” gives the team a clearer direction.

Record the target audience, offer, channel, campaign dates, budget, intended action and measurement method before launch. This creates a reference point for the final report.

2. Choose a small set of meaningful metrics

Use metrics that correspond to the stage of the customer journey and the goal being pursued.

  • Awareness: Reach, impressions, video views and relevant audience growth can indicate how widely content was distributed. They do not by themselves show understanding or purchase intent.
  • Engagement: Meaningful comments, saves, shares, email clicks and time spent with useful content can show whether people interacted. Interpret each measure in the context of the channel.
  • Website activity: Relevant landing-page visits, engaged sessions, navigation to service information and return visits can help assess interest.
  • Lead generation: Enquiry submissions, booked consultations, qualified leads and cost per qualified lead help connect campaigns to sales activity.
  • Sales: Orders, conversion rate, average order value, revenue and contribution after relevant costs help assess commercial outcomes.
  • Retention: Repeat purchases, renewal, repeat enquiries and customer retention can show whether activity supports longer-term relationships.

Avoid reporting every available number. Select a few primary measures and use supporting metrics to explain them.

3. Define what counts as a conversion

A conversion is the action that matters for the campaign. It could be a completed purchase, a qualified enquiry, a consultation booking, a download of a useful resource or a confirmed registration. Define it before collecting results.

Distinguish between a simple action and a valuable outcome. A form submission may be a lead, but it may not be qualified. A product added to a basket is not the same as a completed order. A social media follow is not automatically a sales opportunity.

Where possible, document the stages between first contact and final outcome. This helps the team understand where prospective customers stop progressing and where improvements may be needed.

4. Set up tracking carefully

Use consistent campaign names and tagged links so that website activity can be associated with the relevant campaign. Keep a simple naming convention for source, medium, campaign and content. For example, an organisation might distinguish a newsletter from a paid social campaign even when both link to the same landing page.

Check that analytics and conversion events are configured correctly before launch. Test forms, thank-you pages, checkout steps and contact links. Where a third-party platform is involved, confirm that its reporting definitions match the business question.

Respect privacy requirements. Explain relevant analytics and advertising technologies in the privacy notice, obtain consent where required, configure consent choices appropriately and avoid collecting unnecessary personal information. Do not place personal or sensitive information in analytics URLs or event labels.

5. Establish a baseline and comparison period

A result is easier to interpret when compared with a meaningful baseline. Depending on the campaign, that may be the previous period, a similar campaign, a defined target or performance before a change was introduced.

Make comparisons fairly. Check whether the periods have similar lengths, seasonality, budgets, audience sizes, offers and channel conditions. If several factors changed at once, it may not be possible to attribute the difference to one specific action.

For a new channel or offer, there may be no reliable historical baseline. In that case, record the starting conditions and treat the first campaign as a learning test rather than presenting early numbers as proof of long-term performance.

6. Calculate the measures consistently

Agree on definitions before comparing reports. Common calculations include:

  • Click-through rate (CTR): Clicks divided by impressions, multiplied by 100. Check the platform's definition of a click and impression.
  • Conversion rate: Conversions divided by the relevant visits, clicks or other defined opportunities, multiplied by 100. State which denominator is being used.
  • Cost per lead: Campaign cost divided by the number of leads generated. For better decision-making, also calculate cost per qualified lead.
  • Return on ad spend (ROAS): Attributed revenue divided by advertising spend. ROAS is not the same as profit because it does not automatically include product costs, labour, overhead or other expenses.
  • Customer acquisition cost (CAC): Relevant acquisition costs divided by the number of new customers acquired during the defined period. State which costs are included.

Write the formula and reporting period alongside the result. Different platforms may use different attribution windows or counting rules, so figures from separate systems may not match exactly.

7. Understand attribution limits

Customers may encounter a business through several channels before taking action. They might see a social post, search for the organisation later, read an article and then respond to an email. Attribution is the method used to assign credit to marketing touchpoints.

A last-click report gives credit to the final tracked interaction before a conversion. Other models distribute credit differently. Neither should be treated as a complete account of why a person made a decision. Some influences—such as offline recommendations, private messages or untracked browsing—may not appear in analytics.

Use attribution reports as directional evidence. Where possible, compare them with customer feedback, sales records and campaign timing. Avoid claiming that one channel caused all revenue simply because it received the final tracked click.

8. Turn the report into an improvement plan

A useful report answers four questions: What was the objective? What happened? What might explain the result? What will the team do next?

Separate observed results from interpretation. For example, “the landing page received more visits” is an observation. “The new headline caused the increase” is a causal explanation that needs stronger evidence, such as a controlled test or other supporting analysis.

Choose one or two changes to test. These might include clarifying the offer, improving the landing-page form, changing the audience, testing a different message or adjusting the campaign schedule. Record what changed and review the results after an appropriate period.

9. A simple monthly marketing scorecard

A small organisation can begin with a one-page scorecard. It does not need an expensive dashboard to make better decisions.

  • Objective: The business outcome the activity was intended to support.
  • Activity: What was published, sent, promoted or tested.
  • Audience: Who the activity was intended to reach.
  • Primary result: The most important metric linked to the objective.
  • Quality indicator: A measure such as qualified leads, completed orders or relevant engagement.
  • Cost: Spend and other material costs included in the analysis.
  • Learning: What the team learned, including unexpected or negative results.
  • Next action: What to continue, stop or test in the next period.
Before launching a campaign: Write down the objective, target audience, offer, primary conversion, supporting metrics, tracking method, budget, campaign period and the person responsible for reviewing the results.

Common measurement mistakes

  • Using followers, impressions or website visits as a substitute for business outcomes.
  • Changing metric definitions between reports without explaining the difference.
  • Comparing campaigns with different audiences, budgets or time periods as if they were identical.
  • Counting unqualified enquiries as confirmed customers.
  • Calling ROAS profit or ignoring fulfilment and operating costs.
  • Assuming an attribution report proves that a channel caused a sale.
  • Collecting analytics data without appropriate privacy information and controls.
  • Producing reports without a clear decision or next action.

Measurement should be agreed at the start of a project so that the client and delivery team understand the objective, the relevant indicators and the limits of the available data.

Depending on the agreed scope, reporting may review campaign activity, website engagement, enquiry quality and progress towards the client's stated goal. Results should be described accurately and in context; no metric guarantees future sales or a particular business outcome.

Monthly marketing scorecard template

Choose a small number of measures tied to the objective. Record the source and comparison period so the figures can be interpreted consistently.

  • Reach: qualified website sessions or relevant audience reached.
  • Engagement: meaningful content interactions, email clicks or returning visitors.
  • Conversion: completed enquiries, bookings, purchases or other defined actions.
  • Quality: qualified leads divided by all leads, using a consistent qualification rule.
  • Economics: campaign spend divided by attributed conversions; report attribution limitations.
  • Outcome: revenue or contribution margin where reliable transaction data exists.

Report row: Metric ___ | previous period ___ | current period ___ | target ___ | data source ___ | explanation ___ | next action ___.

Example interpretation: If traffic rises but qualified enquiries do not, inspect audience relevance, landing-page clarity, offer fit and form friction before simply increasing spend. Do not claim a campaign caused revenue unless the tracking and comparison support that conclusion.
